# Chapter 8 — Synthesis: What the Panel Shows and What It Cannot

## 8.1 Purpose of this chapter

The six analytic chapters each examined one facet of a 20-year,
state-by-category panel of IDEA Part B child counts: the national rate
(Chapter 2), category composition (Chapter 3), autism (Chapter 4),
cross-state variation (Chapter 5), placement (Chapter 6), and the pandemic
(Chapter 7). This closing chapter does two things. First, it assembles the
separate findings into a single picture of how American special-education
identification changed between SY2005-06 and SY2024-25. Second — and more
important for a descriptive study — it consolidates the measurement
limits that recurred across every chapter, so that the dataset's reach and
its boundaries are stated once, plainly, in one place.

## 8.2 The empirical picture in one page

Five movements, each established on the data and cross-checked against
external sources, summarize two decades.

1. **The national rate traced a shallow U and then climbed to a record.**
   Identification fell from 13.7% (SY2005-06) to a 12.9% trough around
   SY2012-13, then rose to 15.7% by SY2023-24 (Chapter 2). Enrollment was
   nearly flat throughout; the rate is moved almost entirely by the
   numerator — the count of children served.

2. **The category mix was reorganized.** Specific learning disability fell
   from 45% to 34% of the school-age caseload while autism rose from 3% to
   15% and other-health-impairment from 9% to 16% (Chapter 3). Separating
   shares from counts showed SLD's decline is mostly relative dilution (its
   count fell only ~9%), while intellectual disability and emotional
   disturbance contracted in absolute terms.

3. **Autism grew five-fold and remains an administrative, not
   epidemiological, measure.** The school-age autism rate rose from 3.9 to
   20.3 per 1,000, tracking below but parallel to CDC ADDM estimates, and
   varied 3.6-fold across states in a single year (Chapter 4) — a spread no
   biological prevalence can produce.

4. **State identification rates differ ~1.85-fold and the ordering is
   durable** (Spearman ρ = 0.79 across 19 years), with Texas's
   administrative cap the clearest case of policy driving the number
   (Chapter 5).

5. **Inclusion rose steadily but unevenly.** The share of school-age
   students in the regular classroom ≥80% of the day rose from 61% to 68%,
   but ranges from ~89% (speech/language) to ~21–25% (intellectual and
   multiple disabilities) by category, and autism's inclusion barely moved
   despite its growth (Chapter 6).

6. **COVID-19 produced an artifactual rate rise.** The SY2020-21 increase
   was a denominator shock (enrollment −2.8%, served +0.3%); beneath the
   flat national numerator, new identification fell sharply (Chapter 7).

Three through-lines connect these. The numerator drives the rate
(enrollment is a quiet denominator except in the pandemic). Aggregate
stability hides categorical and geographic churn (the flat-ish rate of the
early 2010s sat atop a major SLD→AUT/OHI reshuffle). And administrative
measurement — eligibility practice, criteria, reporting changes — shapes
every series at least as much as any change in children.

## 8.3 The measurement limits, consolidated

The same cautions surfaced in chapter after chapter. Collected, they define
how far any claim from this dataset may be pushed.

**The rate is an upward-biased estimate, and the bias varies by state.**
The numerator (IDEA served, ages 3–21, including 19–21 transition services
and private-school ISP children) does not match the denominator
(public-school PK–12). Both mismatches push the rate up, do not cancel, and
differ across states — so levels overstate the true PK–12 share, and
state-to-state gaps partly reflect differences in the bias rather than in
identification (Chapters 1, 5).

**Composition must be read within a fixed age band.** The synthesized
all-ages cell does not equal the sum of categories, because the
`ALL_INCOMPLETE_BAND` rule drops categories with a suppressed component
band. All composition analysis was therefore done within the 6–21 band
(Chapters 1, 3); we adopted this as an explicit data-construction rule
before the analysis began.

**Several series have seams that mimic real change.** The NCES denominator
changes edition at SY2011-12 (a vintage seam at the U's trough); the age-5
reclassification at SY2020-21 shifts five-year-olds into the school-age
band, inflating SLI and DD; and these can coincide with genuine shocks
(COVID lands on the age-5 seam in SY2020-21). Each was netted out where it
appeared (Chapters 2, 3, 7).

**The environment fact is less complete than the counts.** In SY2019-20,
34 of 51 states report no LRE data, so that year is excluded from all
placement aggregates; our initial assumption of complete coverage did not
hold there, and we document the gap (Chapter 6).

**The dataset is aggregate and child-anonymous.** It has no individual
identifiers, no diagnostic detail, no flow (only annual stocks), and no
controls for achievement or socioeconomic status. This single fact bounds
the strongest claims in this study: it cannot test diagnostic
substitution (Chapter 3), cannot decompose the autism rise into prevalence
versus practice (Chapter 4), cannot adjudicate over- versus
under-identification (Chapters 2, 5), cannot separate catch-up from new
identification after COVID (Chapter 7), and cannot adjust inclusion rates
for caseload severity (Chapter 6).

**Key caveats live outside the data.** The most policy-relevant
explanations — the Texas cap, the Wisconsin suppression, the COVID
enrollment shock, NCES prekindergarten imputation — are documented in
external sources, not in the data-quality ledger, which
carries only 11 rows under three issue codes (Chapter 1). An analyst
working from the data alone would not be warned of any of them.

## 8.4 A note on normative readings

Because identification is a service that children either receive or are
denied, every number in this study invites a normative reading: is a
high rate over-identification, a low rate neglect, a rising rate progress?
The consistent answer is that **this dataset cannot say.** A low rate can
be an illegal cap denying services (Texas) or an appropriate reflection of
a state's population; a high autism count can be true prevalence, broadened
criteria, or relabeling; a rising national rate can be unmet need finally
met or over-identification. The scholarly disputes the chapters cited —
Morgan and Farkas versus the traditional over-identification view, Shattuck
versus Nelson on diagnostic substitution — turn precisely on the
individual-level controls this aggregate panel lacks. The dataset is an
excellent instrument for describing *what* changed, *where*, and *by how
much*. It is silent on *whether the change was right*, and a descriptive
study should leave that silence intact rather than fill it with the
direction of a trend line.

## 8.5 What the panel is good for

Stated positively, the dataset reliably supports: national and state trends
in identification and placement over two decades; category composition and
its shifts within a fixed age band; cross-state and cross-category
comparison of magnitudes and their persistence; and the separation of
numerator from denominator effects, which is its single most clarifying
analytical move. Used with the seams netted out, the biases acknowledged,
the defective cells excluded, and the normative questions left to
designs that can answer them, it is a sound foundation for descriptive
analysis of how special education in the United States changed between 2005
and 2025 — which is exactly, and only, what it was built to be.

## 8.6 Closing

The recurring lesson of the six analytic chapters is that the interesting
findings and the measurement traps are the same objects seen from two
sides. The flat early-2010s rate *was* a category reshuffle; the COVID rate
rise *was* an enrollment collapse; the autism surge *was* a question about
administrative versus epidemiological counting; the state gaps *were* a
question about whose bias and whose policy. Reading the panel well meant, in
every chapter, refusing to take the headline number at face value and
decomposing it until the mechanism — or the limit — became visible. That
discipline, more than any single statistic, is what this study has
tried to demonstrate.
